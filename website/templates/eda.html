About the Dataset

The Ames Housing dataset was compiled by Dean De Cock and describes the sale of individual residential properties in Ames, Iowa from 2006 to 2010. It contains 1,460 training observations with 80 explanatory variables covering almost every aspect of residential homes — from the number of bathrooms to the type of foundation and the neighborhood.

The target variable is SalePrice, which represents the property's sale price in dollars. Features are split into 37 numerical (e.g., LotArea, GrLivArea, YearBuilt) and 43 categorical (e.g., Neighborhood, HouseStyle, SaleCondition) variables.

Exploratory Data Analysis

Sale Price Distribution

The sale price distribution is right-skewed, with most properties selling between $100,000 and $250,000. A small number of luxury properties extend the tail beyond $500,000. This skewness is important because many ML models assume normally distributed targets.

Why This Matters

We apply a log transformation (np.log1p) to the target variable to normalize the distribution. This reduces the influence of extreme outliers on the model and improves prediction accuracy. The reverse transform (np.expm1) is applied when generating final price predictions.

Correlation Heatmap — Top 15 Features

This heatmap shows the Pearson correlation coefficients between the top 15 features most correlated with SalePrice. OverallQual (0.79) and GrLivArea (0.71) emerge as the strongest predictors.

Key Insight

Notice that GarageCars and GarageArea are highly correlated with each other (0.88), indicating multicollinearity. Similarly, TotRmsAbvGrd is strongly correlated with GrLivArea. When features are highly correlated, they carry redundant information — this motivates our feature engineering approach where we create composite features like TotalSF.

Missing Value Analysis

19 columns contain missing values. The most severe are PoolQC (99.5% missing), MiscFeature (96.3%), Alley (93.8%), and Fence (80.8%). However, many of these aren't truly "missing" — they indicate the absence of a feature (e.g., no pool, no fence).

Handling Strategy

For categorical features where missing means "absence," we fill with "None". For LotFrontage, we use the neighborhood median since neighboring properties tend to have similar lot frontages. Numerical features like MasVnrArea and GarageYrBlt are filled with 0 when the feature doesn't exist.

Sale Price vs. Above Ground Living Area

A clear positive linear relationship exists between living area and sale price. Larger homes consistently sell for higher prices, validating GrLivArea as a critical predictor. Two notable outliers appear at ~4,500+ sq ft with lower-than-expected prices.

Why This Matters

This strong linear relationship is precisely why Linear Regression performs so well on this dataset. The relationship between living area and price is almost proportional, which is exactly the type of pattern linear models excel at capturing. The outliers could be properties with unusual circumstances (e.g., foreclosures, poor condition despite large area).

Median Sale Price by Overall Quality

Overall quality rating (1–10) shows a near-exponential relationship with sale price. Properties rated 10 sell for over 3× the price of those rated 5. This makes OverallQual the single most important predictor in the dataset.

Feature Engineering Implication

The non-linear growth pattern suggests that combining OverallQual with OverallCond (via multiplication) into an OverallScore feature could capture the interaction effect between quality and condition, providing the model with a richer signal.
